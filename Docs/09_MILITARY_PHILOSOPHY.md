# 1. PURPOSE

The military system of Nomad Wars should emerge from the same world conditions that shape its societies.

The armies are not independent machines existing separately from civilization.

They are an extension of:

- the economy;
- animal husbandry;
- migration;
- geography;
- technology;
- culture;
- environmental adaptation;
- political organization.

The central military principle is:

> **An army is part of a mobile society, not merely a collection of combat units.**

This distinction should influence both worldbuilding and gameplay.

---

# 2. THE ARMY AS PART OF SOCIETY

In a conventional fantasy RTS, the player can often think in terms of:

```text
RESOURCE
    ↓
FACTORY
    ↓
UNIT
    ↓
ARMY
````

Nomad Wars should instead emphasize:

```text
SOCIETY
    ↓
ANIMALS + RESOURCES + PEOPLE
    ↓
MOBILITY + LOGISTICS
    ↓
MILITARY CAPABILITY
    ↓
ARMY
```

The army depends on the same systems that sustain the civilian population.

A loss of:

* horses;
* food;
* transport;
* craftsmen;
* territory;
* access to resources

can therefore weaken military power.

Military strength is not completely independent from the health of the society.

---

# 3. WARFARE AS SURVIVAL

The central conflict of the world originates from environmental pressure and the disruption of the long-established balance between societies.

Different peoples historically adapted to different regions and migration patterns.

When environmental conditions change, their territories and migration routes can overlap.

External forces further destabilize this balance.

As a result, warfare is often connected to:

* access to resources;
* migration routes;
* grazing;
* water;
* animals;
* strategic territory;
* trade;
* survival.

The conflict is therefore not simply:

> "One faction wants to conquer the world."

It is frequently:

> **"Two societies need the same place to survive."**

---

# 4. TERRITORY IS NOT THE ULTIMATE OBJECTIVE

Traditional RTS games often reward permanent territorial expansion.

Nomad Wars should treat territory differently.

For a mobile society, territory is valuable primarily because of what it provides:

* food;
* water;
* animals;
* strategic routes;
* minerals;
* shelter;
* defensive positions;
* trade opportunities.

The player does not necessarily need to permanently occupy every territory.

The strategic question is:

> **Is this territory worth staying for?**

This reinforces the migration philosophy.

---

# 5. MOBILITY AS MILITARY POWER

Mobility is not merely a method of moving the base.

It is a military advantage.

A mobile society can:

* choose where to fight;
* avoid unfavorable battles;
* attack weak positions;
* raid enemy resources;
* relocate after a battle;
* reinforce threatened areas;
* exploit changing environmental conditions.

However, mobility should not make an army universally superior.

Mobile forces can have weaknesses:

* reduced defensive capability;
* weaker fortifications;
* logistical dependence on animals;
* vulnerability during migration;
* reduced effectiveness in certain terrain;
* difficulty protecting large amounts of infrastructure.

The intended design is therefore:

```text
MOBILITY
     ↕
DEFENSE
```

The player constantly balances the two.

---

# 6. THE ARMY AND THE SETTLEMENT

The settlement and army should be connected.

A deployed settlement provides:

* production;
* storage;
* healing or recovery where applicable;
* military infrastructure;
* defensive structures;
* economic stability.

A mobile settlement provides:

* strategic mobility;
* the ability to follow resources;
* the ability to relocate away from danger;
* the ability to establish a new position.

Therefore:

```text
DEPLOYED SETTLEMENT
        ↓
HIGHER STABILITY
        ↓
HIGHER MILITARY EFFICIENCY

MOBILE SETTLEMENT
        ↓
HIGHER FLEXIBILITY
        ↓
LOWER LOCAL EFFICIENCY
```

This creates a meaningful military decision:

> **Do I fortify this position or move?**

---

# 7. MILITARY MOVEMENT

Movement should be treated as a strategic system rather than only a pathfinding problem.

Military movement is influenced by:

* terrain;
* environmental zones;
* settlement state;
* animals;
* unit type;
* army composition;
* enemy presence.

Fast units may operate ahead of the main army.

Heavy units may slow the formation.

Mobile buildings may accompany an army or settlement.

Scouts may move independently.

This creates differences between:

* army speed;
* scout speed;
* cavalry speed;
* settlement speed;
* heavy military equipment speed.

---

# 8. ANIMAL LOGISTICS

Animals have a special military role.

Horses can simultaneously support:

* cavalry;
* scouting;
* transport;
* migration;
* logistics.

Other domesticated animals can support:

* food;
* trade;
* economic production;
* transport;
* survival.

This means animal losses can have military consequences without directly killing soldiers.

For example:

```text
Enemy raid
    ↓
Horse herd captured
    ↓
Cavalry capacity decreases
    ↓
Scouting becomes weaker
    ↓
Migration becomes slower
    ↓
Military flexibility decreases
```

The system should make animals strategically valuable without turning them into an unnecessarily complicated simulation in the MVP.

---

# 9. HORSES AND MILITARY CAPACITY

For factions that depend heavily on mounted warfare, horses represent military potential.

At the beginning of the game, wild horses may have to be captured.

The initial economy can therefore include a phase in which the player establishes sufficient animal resources before developing a strong mounted force.

Conceptually:

```text
WILD HORSES
     ↓
CAPTURE
     ↓
HERD
     ↓
MILITARY CAPACITY
     ↓
CAVALRY
```

The exact numerical relationship between horses and military units belongs to the gameplay balance documents.

The worldbuilding principle remains:

> **Cavalry requires an animal economy.**

---

# 10. PLUNDER AND RAIDING

Warfare in Nomad Wars is not limited to destroying enemy armies.

Raiding can be an economic strategy.

A successful attack can potentially provide:

* resources;
* livestock;
* captured supplies;
* access to enemy infrastructure;
* disruption of enemy production.

This means a weaker army can potentially damage a stronger opponent without defeating the entire enemy settlement.

A player may decide:

> "I cannot destroy their base, but I can destroy their stable and steal their horses."

This creates asymmetric strategic possibilities.

---

# 11. PLUNDER AS A UNIVERSAL MILITARY PRINCIPLE

Plunder should not necessarily be an exclusive racial ability.

The concept can be part of the underlying rules of warfare.

All factions can potentially benefit economically from damaging enemy infrastructure.

Faction identity should instead determine **how effectively or in what manner** a faction exploits this system.

This prevents a single faction from monopolizing an important economic dimension.

---

# 12. CAPTURE OF LIVESTOCK

Livestock can become a military objective.

A successful attack on an animal facility may create an opportunity to capture animals.

Possible gameplay interactions include:

```text
Attack stable
      ↓
Stable damaged / destroyed
      ↓
Animals become vulnerable
      ↓
Suitable units capture animals
      ↓
Animals move toward friendly territory
```

This creates a different objective from simply killing units.

The enemy may therefore need to protect:

* military forces;
* resource structures;
* livestock;
* migration infrastructure.

---

# 13. MILITARY CLASSES

The initial military system can use three broad weight categories.

## LIGHT

Characteristics:

* low armor;
* high mobility;
* relatively low attack strength;
* useful for scouting, harassment and maneuver.

Typical roles:

* scouts;
* skirmishers;
* light cavalry;
* fast ranged units.

---

## MEDIUM

Characteristics:

* balanced armor;
* balanced speed;
* balanced attack.

Typical roles:

* main infantry;
* general-purpose cavalry;
* frontline soldiers;
* standard ranged troops.

Medium units should form the reliable core of an army.

---

## HEAVY

Characteristics:

* high armor;
* high damage;
* low mobility;
* greater logistical burden.

Typical roles:

* heavy infantry;
* heavy cavalry;
* elite warriors;
* specialized frontline units.

Heavy units should provide power at the cost of mobility.

---

# 14. DAMAGE AND COMBAT ROLES

Units can also be differentiated by how they deliver damage.

Primary categories include:

### Melee

Units that must approach the enemy.

Strengths:

* frontline presence;
* reliable damage;
* protection of ranged units.

Weaknesses:

* vulnerable to kiting;
* require contact.

### Ranged

Units that attack from distance.

Strengths:

* battlefield control;
* support;
* harassment.

Weaknesses:

* weaker when surrounded;
* require protection.

### Siege

Specialized units or mechanisms designed primarily against:

* buildings;
* defensive structures;
* concentrated enemy formations.

Examples may include:

* catapult-like systems;
* ballista-like mechanisms;
* faction-specific siege technology.

### Flying

Airborne units that operate independently of ordinary ground movement.

Strengths can include:

* reconnaissance;
* bypassing terrain;
* attacking vulnerable targets;
* rapid movement.

Weaknesses may include:

* specialized counters;
* lower durability;
* resource or technological requirements.

The exact unit roster is defined separately.

---

# 15. FLYING UNITS

The world is explicitly fantasy.

Therefore airborne military units are possible.

Flying creatures and units should not exist merely because the genre permits them.

Their presence should have a place in the world's ecology, mythology, technology or magical systems.

Possible origins include:

* naturally evolved flying creatures;
* domesticated aerial animals;
* magical beings;
* spirit entities;
* engineered or mechanical flying machines;
* faction-specific creatures.

The exact implementation should be faction-specific and world-consistent.

---

# 16. HEROES

Heroes are an important bridge between RTS and RPG-inspired gameplay.

Unlike ordinary units, heroes can possess:

* experience;
* levels;
* abilities;
* equipment;
* artifacts;
* auras;
* specialized roles.

The hero system should allow players to develop a character over the course of a match or campaign.

Heroes should not replace the army.

They should influence the army.

---

# 17. HERO ROLES

Heroes can occupy different battlefield roles.

Examples:

### Warrior

High melee combat capability.

### Tank

High durability and defensive abilities.

### Ranged Hero

High ranged damage and battlefield control.

### Support Hero

Healing, buffs or utility.

### Mobility Hero

High movement speed and scouting capability.

### Caster

Magical damage, control or resource manipulation.

### Commander

Strong auras and army-wide bonuses.

A single hero can combine several roles.

---

# 18. HERO AURAS

Heroes can influence nearby allied units through auras.

An aura represents the hero's:

* leadership;
* morale;
* tactical coordination;
* magical influence;
* spiritual authority.

Auras should reward intelligent positioning.

For example:

```text
Hero
  ↓
Aura radius
  ↓
Nearby allied units
  ↓
Improved combat capability
```

This creates a tactical reason to protect and position heroes correctly.

A hero should therefore be valuable not only because of direct damage.

---

# 19. HERO EQUIPMENT AND ARTIFACTS

Heroes can carry a limited inventory of artifacts.

The initial concept allows approximately nine or more inventory slots, subject to later UX and balance decisions.

Artifacts can modify:

* attack;
* defense;
* movement;
* abilities;
* health;
* mana;
* aura effects;
* resistance;
* specialized abilities.

The purpose is to introduce an RPG-like layer without turning the main RTS into a full inventory-management game.

---

# 20. HEROES AS LONG-TERM CHARACTERS

Heroes can potentially exist beyond a single skirmish.

The same universe can support:

* campaign missions;
* standalone hero maps;
* defense scenarios;
* tower-defense-like maps;
* hero-focused gameplay;
* future spin-off games;
* DLC;
* alternative game modes.

The shared foundation is the world, characters and gameplay systems.

The RTS therefore functions not only as one game but potentially as the foundation for a broader universe.

This possibility should not increase MVP scope.

It should instead influence architectural decisions so that the hero system is not unnecessarily hard-coded into one game mode.

---

# 21. MILITARY TECHNOLOGY

Technology should emerge from faction identity.

Different societies should not simply use different skins for identical technology.

Their military systems should reflect:

* available materials;
* environment;
* culture;
* economy;
* architecture;
* animals;
* magical traditions;
* historical inspirations transformed into fantasy.

For example, one faction may emphasize:

* mounted warfare;
* mobile structures;
* practical mechanisms.

Another may emphasize:

* elite warriors;
* fire;
* prestige materials;
* animal-style weapons.

Another may emphasize:

* heavy formations;
* robust equipment;
* mass military organization.

---

# 22. MAGIC AND MILITARY

Magic exists in the fantasy world but should not erase the importance of ordinary military systems.

Magic may influence:

* heroes;
* specialized units;
* support;
* environmental interaction;
* defenses;
* siege;
* reconnaissance.

For example, magical towers may specialize in damaging:

* magical units;
* shamans;
* spirit-based units;
* heroes with high mana dependence.

Magic should therefore create additional strategic layers rather than simply being a stronger form of physical damage.

---

# 23. DEFENSIVE STRUCTURES

Defensive buildings are part of the mobile-settlement philosophy.

Some towers can move.

However, their combat effectiveness should change between states.

### DEPLOYED

* maximum attack efficiency;
* higher stability;
* stronger defensive capability.

### MOBILE

* reduced attack rate;
* reduced efficiency;
* slower movement;
* potentially limited targeting.

This creates a trade-off between:

> **fortification and mobility.**

Different tower types may specialize in:

1. standard defense;
2. light / rapid defense;
3. heavy / slow defense;
4. magical defense.

---

# 24. MOBILE SIEGE CONCEPT

Some mobile defensive structures can potentially be used offensively.

A player may relocate a tower toward an enemy settlement and deploy it near the enemy.

This creates a hybrid between:

* settlement defense;
* mobile fortification;
* siege support.

The mechanic should remain consistent with the faction's technological and architectural identity.

It should not simply copy Warcraft's Ancient Protectors or other existing units.

---

# 25. ARMY COMPOSITION

Because movement is important, army composition matters.

A player who creates an army containing too many heavy units may gain combat strength but lose strategic mobility.

A highly mobile army may:

* raid;
* scout;
* retreat;
* relocate quickly.

But it may struggle against:

* fortified positions;
* heavy enemy formations;
* siege;
* prolonged combat.

Therefore the optimal army is context-dependent.

---

# 26. ENVIRONMENT AND WARFARE

Environmental zones affect military strategy.

Potential effects include:

### Favorable region

* normal or improved movement;
* strong supply;
* healthy animals;
* good visibility depending on terrain.

### Drought

Potential effects:

* reduced food;
* reduced animal sustainability;
* increased logistical pressure.

### Severe cold

Potential effects:

* reduced movement;
* reduced production;
* increased survival pressure.

### Transition regions

Potentially provide intermediate conditions.

The exact numerical modifiers belong to gameplay documents.

---

# 27. MILITARY AND MIGRATION

Military operations and migration should influence one another.

A player may use an army to:

* secure a migration route;
* protect livestock;
* scout the destination;
* defend workers;
* establish a forward position.

Conversely, an army may need to migrate because:

* food disappears;
* animals move;
* the settlement relocates;
* environmental conditions deteriorate.

Thus military strategy cannot be completely separated from settlement strategy.

---

# 28. WAR WITHOUT TOTAL CONQUEST

Nomad Wars should support military objectives other than complete annihilation.

Possible objectives include:

* force enemy migration;
* capture resources;
* steal livestock;
* destroy production;
* control a strategic route;
* protect one's own migration;
* eliminate an enemy army;
* occupy a temporary strategic location.

This makes warfare more compatible with the mobile nature of the world.

---

# 29. CONFLICT EMERGENCE

The preferred model is emergent conflict.

The game should not always need a scripted reason for two armies to fight.

Instead:

```text
Environmental shift
       ↓
Migration
       ↓
Territorial overlap
       ↓
Resource competition
       ↓
Military contact
       ↓
Player decision
       ↓
Conflict
```

The environment can therefore act as an indirect conflict generator.

---

# 30. STRATEGIC IDENTITY OF NOMAD WARS

The military philosophy can be summarized through several principles.

### Mobility

Move when the world requires it.

### Adaptation

Build an army appropriate to the environment.

### Logistics

An army requires an economy and animals.

### Information

Knowing where resources and environmental changes are heading is valuable.

### Raiding

Military action can generate economic value.

### Positioning

Terrain and migration routes matter.

### Timing

The strongest army in the wrong place can still lose.

### Adaptability

A society that can reorganize after environmental change has a strategic advantage.

---

# 31. MILITARY DESIGN PILLARS

The following pillars should guide future military design.

## Pillar 1 — Mobility

The ability to move is a major strategic resource.

## Pillar 2 — Logistics

Armies depend on the economic and animal systems supporting them.

## Pillar 3 — Adaptation

No army composition should be universally optimal.

## Pillar 4 — Positioning

Where an army fights can matter as much as what units it contains.

## Pillar 5 — Economy

Warfare can create economic consequences and opportunities.

## Pillar 6 — Information

Scouting and environmental awareness influence strategic decisions.

## Pillar 7 — Cultural Identity

Each faction's military should express its society.

---

# 32. MVP MILITARY PHILOSOPHY

The MVP does not need to implement the entire military vision.

The minimum system should demonstrate:

```text
ONE FACTION
    +
MELEE
    +
RANGED
    +
LIGHT / MEDIUM / HEAVY DIFFERENCES
    +
BASIC CAVALRY
    +
HERO
    +
MOBILE SETTLEMENT
    +
BASIC RAID / PLUNDER
    +
HORSE ECONOMY
```

Flying units, advanced siege, extensive hero inventories, complex magic and multiple factions can be added later.

The MVP must first prove that the combination of:

> **mobility + economy + environment + military**

creates a compelling RTS loop.

Once the foundation is stable, the military system can expand into:

* more factions;
* faction-specific cavalry;
* flying units;
* siege units;
* magical units;
* advanced heroes;
* hero equipment;
* campaign heroes;
* faction-specific auras;
* livestock capture;
* advanced raiding;
* naval warfare;
* special environmental units;
* legendary creatures;
* additional game modes.

These systems should be layered onto the existing foundations rather than forcing a complete redesign.

# 34. DESIGN RULES

The following rules should remain stable unless deliberately revised.

### Rule 1

The army is part of society.

### Rule 2

Military power depends on logistics.

### Rule 3

Mobility is a strategic advantage, not a universal advantage.

### Rule 4

Heavy military power should have a mobility cost.

### Rule 5

Animals can have economic and military value simultaneously.

### Rule 6

Horses can represent cavalry and logistical capacity.

### Rule 7

Raiding and plunder can be legitimate strategic objectives.

### Rule 8

Territory matters because of what it provides.

### Rule 9

Heroes influence armies rather than completely replacing them.

### Rule 10

Different factions should express their cultures through military systems.

### Rule 11

Magic should create additional tactical possibilities rather than invalidate conventional warfare.

### Rule 12

Environmental change should influence military decisions.

### Rule 13

Military conflict should often emerge from competition for resources and migration routes.

### Rule 14

MVP implementation should remain substantially simpler than the complete world vision.

# 36. FINAL PRINCIPLE

Nomad Wars should not portray war as an isolated contest between armies.

War is a consequence of societies attempting to survive in a changing world.

The complete relationship is:

```text
WORLD
  ↓
ENVIRONMENT
  ↓
RESOURCES
  ↓
MIGRATION
  ↓
SOCIETY
  ↓
LOGISTICS
  ↓
ARMY
  ↓
CONFLICT
  ↓
RESOURCE LOSS / GAIN
  ↓
MIGRATION
  ↓
WORLD CHANGES AGAIN
```

The army therefore exists inside the same cycle as the rest of society.

A player should not ask only:

> **"How strong is my army?"**

They should also ask:

> **"Can my society sustain this army?"**

> **"Where will my army need to be when the environment changes?"**

> **"Should I fight, raid, defend, or migrate?"**

> **"Is winning this battle actually worth staying here?"**

The intended military identity of Nomad Wars is therefore:

> **Do not simply conquer the land. Survive the land, move through it, exploit it, defend your people, and decide when war is worth fighting.**
