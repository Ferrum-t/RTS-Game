# 11 — WORLD CONFLICT

**Project:** Nomad Wars  
**Document type:** World Foundation / Worldbuilding  
**Status:** Canon — foundational design  
**Scope:** Origin of the central conflict, political pressure, migration conflicts, external threats and long-term narrative framework

---

## 1. PURPOSE OF THIS DOCUMENT

This document defines the fundamental conflict of the world of **Nomad Wars**.

It explains:

- why the major civilizations are in conflict;
- why migration becomes unavoidable;
- why neighboring peoples begin entering one another's territories;
- why resources become the primary strategic concern;
- what destabilized the former balance;
- what external forces may be responsible;
- how the central conflict can support RTS gameplay;
- how the world can later expand into campaigns, additional factions and standalone games.

This document does not define individual wars, battles or campaign missions.

Those belong to later narrative and game-design documents.

---

# 2. THE WORLD BEFORE THE CONFLICT

For many generations, the major civilizations existed within a relatively stable system.

The world was never peaceful in the absolute sense.

There were:

- raids;
- territorial disputes;
- rivalries;
- local wars;
- competition for resources;
- political conflicts.

However, these conflicts existed inside a broader ecological and cultural equilibrium.

The major nomadic civilizations had learned how to survive within the changing world.

They understood:

- seasonal movement;
- migration routes;
- water sources;
- grazing territories;
- animal populations;
- dangerous regions;
- climatic cycles;
- neighboring peoples.

Their societies developed traditions and rules that allowed them to move without constantly destroying the ecological basis of their own existence.

The world was difficult.

But it was predictable enough.

---

# 3. THE GREAT BALANCE

The civilizations of the known continent developed different ecological niches.

Some populations occupied:

- northern cold regions;
- tundra;
- forest-steppe;
- open steppe;
- semi-deserts;
- deserts;
- mountain valleys;
- river systems;
- coastal territories.

Their migration patterns were not random.

Each culture developed its own traditional routes.

These routes became part of:

- culture;
- economy;
- religion;
- political organization;
- military strategy.

The movement of one civilization was therefore indirectly connected with the movement of others.

For centuries, this system functioned.

---

# 4. THE FUNDAMENTAL PROBLEM

The central problem of the world is not simply:

> "Who owns the land?"

The deeper problem is:

> **There is never enough favorable land for everyone at the same time.**

The environment changes.

The productive zones move.

Water availability changes.

Animal populations migrate.

Grasslands recover and disappear.

Cold and drought alter the usefulness of entire regions.

Therefore, territory is not equally valuable at all times.

A region that is almost useless during one period may become extremely valuable during another.

---

# 5. THE MOVING WORLD

The environment is one of the principal forces of the world.

The favorable zones move across the continent.

A simplified representation is:

```text
GREEN
Favorable conditions
      ↓
YELLOW
Transition
      ↓
RED
Drought / heat / scarcity
      ↓
BLUE
Cold / winter / reduced productivity
````

These zones are not necessarily literal geometric squares.

Their actual shapes are determined by:

* geography;
* rivers;
* mountains;
* forests;
* deserts;
* climate;
* seasonal cycles.

The movement of these zones creates continuous pressure on civilization.

---

# 6. MIGRATION AS SURVIVAL

Migration is therefore not simply a cultural preference.

For many civilizations it is a survival mechanism.

When conditions deteriorate:

* animals move;
* herds move;
* people move;
* settlements move;
* military forces move.

A civilization that refuses to move may lose:

* food;
* livestock;
* horses;
* water;
* production;
* population;
* military capacity.

Therefore:

> **The ability to move is itself a strategic resource.**

---

# 7. THE OLD RULES OF MIGRATION

The major cultures traditionally followed established migration systems.

These could include:

* seasonal routes;
* grazing territories;
* water rights;
* temporary settlements;
* agreements between tribes;
* trade routes;
* neutral territories;
* sacred locations.

These rules prevented every environmental change from becoming a war.

A civilization did not necessarily need to conquer its neighbor.

It could simply move according to an established route.

---

# 8. WHAT CHANGED

The ancient balance is disrupted by an external event.

The exact cause remains a major part of the developing mythology.

Possible explanations include:

* an ancient evil awakening;
* the arrival of peoples from across the ocean;
* a supernatural event;
* a change in the world's climate;
* a magical disturbance;
* the collapse of an ancient system;
* several events occurring simultaneously.

The final explanation should be determined later.

The important established fact is:

> **The old balance no longer works.**

---

# 9. THE PRESSURE CASCADE

Once one civilization is displaced, the effects propagate through the continent.

The basic chain is:

```text
ENVIRONMENTAL CHANGE
        ↓
RESOURCE SCARCITY
        ↓
MIGRATION
        ↓
TRADITIONAL TERRITORY OCCUPIED
        ↓
NEIGHBORING POPULATION DISPLACED
        ↓
SECONDARY MIGRATION
        ↓
RESOURCE COMPETITION
        ↓
RAIDS
        ↓
WAR
```

The conflict therefore does not require every civilization to suddenly become aggressive.

They may simply be reacting to circumstances.

---

# 10. WAR IS A CONSEQUENCE

This is one of the central philosophical principles of Nomad Wars.

The major civilizations do not necessarily fight because they hate one another.

They fight because the conditions that previously allowed them to coexist are breaking down.

A civilization may invade another's territory because:

* its livestock are starving;
* its water sources have disappeared;
* its migration route has been blocked;
* another population has entered its traditional territory;
* its horses are dying;
* its people have nowhere else to go.

From the perspective of the invading civilization:

> it may not consider itself the aggressor.

It may consider itself desperate.

The same event can therefore be interpreted differently by both sides.

---

# 11. RESOURCE CONFLICT

Resources become the immediate cause of many conflicts.

Important resources include:

* food;
* water;
* wood;
* stone;
* metal;
* gold;
* animals;
* horses;
* magical resources.

However, not every resource behaves identically.

Some resources are:

### Permanent

Examples:

* gold deposits;
* major mineral deposits;
* stone formations.

### Seasonal

Examples:

* grass;
* certain plants;
* temporary water sources;
* animal populations.

### Mobile

Examples:

* wild horses;
* migrating herds;
* certain magical creatures.

This creates different forms of strategic competition.

---

# 12. HORSES AND THE CONFLICT

Horses are particularly important.

They represent:

* transportation;
* cavalry;
* logistics;
* migration capacity;
* military expansion.

A civilization that loses access to horses may become less mobile.

Therefore, horse herds can become strategic objectives.

Players may:

* capture wild horses;
* protect their own herds;
* raid enemy animal facilities;
* steal horses;
* transport horses;
* recover captured animals.

This makes animal resources part of warfare rather than merely an economic statistic.

---

# 13. RAIDING

Raiding is an important form of warfare.

A raid does not necessarily aim to destroy an enemy civilization.

It may aim to:

* steal resources;
* capture livestock;
* damage infrastructure;
* destroy production;
* interrupt migration;
* force an enemy to relocate;
* weaken an enemy before a larger conflict.

This reflects the realities of a mobile economy.

A civilization can damage an opponent's ability to survive without conquering its entire territory.

---

# 14. PLUNDER

The world's military philosophy allows **plunder** to be integrated directly into gameplay.

Military units may gain resources by damaging enemy structures.

This represents:

* destruction of stored resources;
* capture of goods;
* dismantling valuable materials;
* livestock recovery;
* seizure of supplies.

The mechanic should not be understood as a simple reward system.

It represents a real economic relationship between warfare and survival.

---

# 15. CAPTURED LIVESTOCK

Some structures may contain animals.

For example:

* stables;
* animal enclosures;
* temporary camps.

If such a structure is destroyed or captured, its animals may become available to the attacker.

A later technology or ability may allow workers or specialized units to capture and transport them.

This creates an additional strategic decision:

> Destroy the enemy infrastructure, or attempt to seize its economic assets?

---

# 16. TERRITORY

Territory in Nomad Wars should not be understood only as permanent ownership.

A territory has changing value.

Its usefulness depends on:

* climate;
* season;
* water;
* resources;
* animal populations;
* migration patterns;
* strategic position.

A player may therefore control a region that becomes less valuable later.

Conversely, a previously unimportant region may become strategically critical.

---

# 17. MOBILE SETTLEMENTS AND WAR

Because major settlements can move, territorial warfare becomes different from conventional RTS warfare.

The enemy's headquarters is not necessarily permanently fixed.

A settlement may:

1. operate in a deployed state;
2. receive warnings about environmental deterioration;
3. pack itself;
4. begin migration;
5. travel toward a new region;
6. deploy again.

This creates the possibility of:

* pursuit;
* interception;
* ambush;
* defensive migration;
* strategic retreat;
* attacks on moving settlements.

---

# 18. THE COST OF MOBILITY

Mobility is not absolute freedom.

A moving settlement is generally:

* slower;
* less productive;
* less defensible;
* less efficient in combat.

A deployed settlement is:

* stronger;
* more productive;
* more defensible;
* better suited for production and combat.

Therefore:

> **Every migration creates a temporary period of vulnerability.**

An enemy can exploit that vulnerability.

---

# 19. THE STRATEGIC PARADOX

This creates one of the core strategic tensions of the game:

> **You must move to survive, but moving makes you vulnerable.**

Staying too long:

* exhausts resources;
* exposes the settlement to environmental deterioration.

Moving too early:

* sacrifices production;
* exposes the settlement to attack;
* may lead into another dangerous region.

The player must therefore constantly evaluate:

**When should we stay?**

**When should we move?**

**Where should we go?**

---

# 20. CONFLICT BETWEEN THE FOUR MAJOR CULTURES

The major civilizations share the same world but have different adaptations.

The current major cultures are:

1. Andronovo-inspired northern civilization;
2. Saka-inspired civilization;
3. Hunnic-inspired civilization;
4. Turan / broader Eurasian nomadic civilization.

These are fantasy civilizations.

They are inspired by historical cultures and visual traditions but are not intended to be direct historical reconstructions.

Their conflict should arise from:

* geography;
* environmental pressure;
* cultural differences;
* political interests;
* resources;
* migration routes;
* external events.

---

# 21. NORTHERN PRESSURE

The northern regions contain:

* tundra;
* cold steppe;
* harsh seasonal conditions;
* large cold-adapted animals.

The civilizations living there are adapted to these conditions.

If environmental conditions change or external forces push populations southward, these populations may enter territories traditionally occupied by other peoples.

This can create a chain reaction across the continent.

---

# 22. CENTRAL PRESSURE

Central regions contain extensive steppe and transitional environments.

They can become major migration corridors.

Because these regions connect different ecological zones, they are strategically important.

Control of the central steppe can influence:

* migration;
* trade;
* military movement;
* livestock;
* access to water;
* communication between civilizations.

---

# 23. SOUTHERN AND EASTERN PRESSURE

Southern and eastern regions contain combinations of:

* steppe;
* semi-desert;
* desert;
* mountains;
* fertile valleys;
* river systems.

These environments create different forms of nomadism.

Some populations may be highly mobile.

Others may rely on:

* mountain pastures;
* river valleys;
* seasonal agricultural settlements;
* trade networks.

The interaction between these lifestyles creates additional sources of conflict.

---

# 24. SEDENTARY CIVILIZATIONS

Not every civilization is nomadic.

There are smaller sedentary states and settlements.

They may exist around:

* rivers;
* fertile agricultural zones;
* mountains;
* trade routes;
* coastal regions.

In gameplay they can function as:

* neutral settlements;
* markets;
* taverns;
* trading posts;
* mercenary camps;
* quest locations;
* sources of information;
* independent factions.

They may sometimes become allies or enemies.

---

# 25. ISLAND AND MARITIME POWERS

The known world also contains:

* islands;
* coastal states;
* maritime civilizations.

These civilizations developed differently because their survival depends more heavily on:

* fishing;
* shipping;
* coastal resources;
* maritime trade;
* naval technology.

They may become important later in the world's history.

They are not required to be part of the initial RTS MVP.

---

# 26. THE GREAT OCEAN

A large portion of the planet is covered by an enormous ocean.

Much of it remains unexplored.

Only a fraction of the world is known with confidence.

Legends describe:

* distant continents;
* unknown civilizations;
* strange creatures;
* ancient ruins;
* lost lands.

The existence of these regions provides a long-term expansion space for the universe.

---

# 27. THE UNKNOWN CONTINENTS

The distant continents should remain deliberately mysterious during the early history of the game.

They may eventually introduce:

* new civilizations;
* new ecological systems;
* new magic;
* new technologies;
* new resources;
* new conflicts.

They may become the foundation for future expansions or separate games.

The main world does not need to explain everything immediately.

---

# 28. THE SOUTHERN HELL-CONTINENT

One particularly extreme continent exists toward the southern polar regions.

It is relatively small compared with the main continent but contains extreme environmental conditions.

Its geography combines:

* polar conditions;
* permanent ice;
* active volcanism;
* extreme cold;
* geothermal regions;
* areas of intense heat.

Some regions may experience temperatures approaching:

**−70°C to −90°C**

while other regions contain:

* lava;
* volcanic activity;
* extreme geothermal environments.

The continent is therefore one of the most hostile regions of the known world.

Its final biological and geological logic remains to be developed.

---

# 29. EXTERNAL THREATS

The disruption of the world may not originate entirely from internal competition.

Potential external threats include:

* civilizations arriving from across the ocean;
* ancient supernatural forces;
* demons;
* undead;
* deep-sea civilizations;
* unknown northern civilizations;
* creatures emerging from previously inaccessible regions.

These threats should be introduced gradually.

The initial conflict between the major civilizations should remain understandable even without requiring the player to know the entire mythology.

---

# 30. THE EXTERNAL INVASION POSSIBILITY

One possible historical explanation is the arrival of an external civilization from beyond the known world.

They may resemble the narrative function of the Orc invasion in classic fantasy:

> a foreign civilization arrives and disrupts the existing balance.

However, the new civilization should not simply be a copy of Warcraft's Orcs.

Their:

* origin;
* motivations;
* biology;
* culture;
* technology;
* magic;
* relationship with the environment

must eventually be developed independently.

The central question is:

> Are they conquerors, refugees, explorers, exiles, or survivors of another catastrophe?

This remains unresolved.

---

# 31. ANCIENT EVIL POSSIBILITY

Another possible explanation is that something ancient has awakened.

It could have existed before the current civilizations.

Its awakening may affect:

* climate;
* magic;
* animals;
* migration;
* resources;
* geography.

The exact nature of this force is intentionally undefined.

It should not be introduced merely as:

> "evil appeared because the story needed a villain."

Its existence should eventually connect to the deeper history of the world.

---

# 32. MULTIPLE CAUSES

The final canon may combine several explanations.

For example:

```text
Environmental instability
        +
Ancient disturbance
        +
External migration
        +
Political failure
        ↓
Collapse of the old balance
        ↓
Mass migration
        ↓
Resource conflicts
        ↓
Regional wars
```

This would allow the world conflict to operate on several levels simultaneously.

---

# 33. LOCAL AND GLOBAL CONFLICT

The player should not necessarily know the complete global explanation at the beginning.

At the gameplay level, a player may simply know:

> "The land is becoming hostile and another people is occupying the territory we need."

At the campaign level, the player may gradually discover:

> "Something is causing the balance to collapse."

At the deepest narrative level, the player may eventually discover:

> "The current crisis is connected to an event much older than any existing civilization."

This creates room for long-term storytelling.

---

# 34. CONFLICT WITHOUT A SINGLE VILLAIN

Nomad Wars does not require the entire world to be divided into:

**good civilization vs evil civilization.**

Different civilizations can have legitimate reasons for their actions.

One faction may:

* invade because it is starving;
* raid because its horses are dying;
* defend a sacred migration route;
* seize a water source;
* attack a settlement because it believes another population caused its crisis.

The player may disagree with the enemy while still understanding its motivation.

---

# 35. WAR AND MORAL AMBIGUITY

The central conflict can therefore explore questions such as:

* What happens when survival requires taking another person's resources?
* When does migration become invasion?
* When does defense become conquest?
* Can a civilization remain peaceful when its environment becomes hostile?
* Who is responsible when everyone is reacting to circumstances?
* Is territory truly owned if nature itself constantly changes it?

These questions belong to the philosophical layer of the project.

They should not necessarily be delivered through dialogue.

They can emerge naturally from gameplay.

---

# 36. THE PLAYER'S POSITION

The player is not the master of the world.

The player controls a civilization attempting to survive within it.

The player can influence:

* settlement position;
* migration;
* resource exploitation;
* military strategy;
* diplomacy;
* raids;
* technology;
* heroes.

But the player cannot completely control:

* climate;
* seasons;
* animal migration;
* resource regeneration;
* distant events;
* the movement of environmental zones.

This reinforces the central philosophy:

> **The world changes the players.**

---

# 37. CONFLICT AS A GAMEPLAY ENGINE

The world conflict should directly support the gameplay loop:

```text
ENVIRONMENT
    ↓
RESOURCE AVAILABILITY
    ↓
MIGRATION PRESSURE
    ↓
SETTLEMENT POSITION
    ↓
ECONOMY
    ↓
MILITARY PREPARATION
    ↓
RAID / DEFEND / ATTACK
    ↓
CAPTURE / PLUNDER
    ↓
RESOURCE CHANGE
    ↓
MIGRATION
```

War is therefore not a separate mode layered onto the world.

It is part of the same ecological-economic system.

---

# 38. THE CENTRAL RTS PHILOSOPHY

Traditional RTS design often assumes:

> Build a base → gather resources → expand → destroy the enemy.

Nomad Wars changes the underlying assumption.

The intended philosophy is:

> **Survive → adapt → migrate → compete → fight → adapt again.**

The player does not simply expand over static territory.

The player follows opportunities created by a changing world.

---

# 39. LONG-TERM NARRATIVE STRUCTURE

The universe is designed to support several scales of storytelling.

### Level 1 — RTS Matches

The player experiences:

* migration;
* resource competition;
* raids;
* settlement movement;
* battles.

### Level 2 — Campaign

The player learns:

* faction histories;
* political relationships;
* heroes;
* major historical events;
* reasons behind the current crisis.

### Level 3 — World Mythology

The player gradually discovers:

* ancient civilizations;
* origins of magic;
* external continents;
* the reason the world's balance changed;
* deeper forces influencing the world.

### Level 4 — Future Games

The same universe can support:

* hero-based games;
* campaign games;
* survival games;
* tactical games;
* strategy expansions;
* new continents;
* new civilizations.

---

# 40. HEROES AS HISTORICAL ACTORS

Heroes can become important beyond individual matches.

A hero may eventually have:

* a personal history;
* a faction;
* allies;
* enemies;
* artifacts;
* unique abilities;
* relationships with other heroes.

A hero introduced in the RTS could later become the protagonist of:

* a campaign;
* a separate map;
* a hero-focused game;
* a future standalone title.

The shared universe should therefore be designed so that gameplay characters can become narrative anchors.

# 42. CURRENT MVP CONFLICT

For the first playable version, the world conflict should remain simple.

The MVP does not need:

* all civilizations;
* the complete global history;
* every external threat;
* all continents;
* naval warfare;
* demons;
* undead;
* deep-sea civilizations.

The MVP needs only enough context to explain:

1. why the Turan civilization exists;
2. why it migrates;
3. why resources matter;
4. why settlements move;
5. why another civilization may become an enemy;
6. why the player must compete for favorable territory.

---

# 43. CURRENT MVP FACTION

The current MVP civilization is provisionally:

**Turan**

Its initial conflict should demonstrate the fundamental game philosophy.

The player should experience:

```text
Favorable territory
       ↓
Resource accumulation
       ↓
Environmental deterioration
       ↓
Migration decision
       ↓
Enemy territory
       ↓
Tension
       ↓
Raid / conflict
       ↓
New territory
       ↓
New environmental cycle
```

This is more important than having a large number of factions.

# 44. CORE WORLD STATEMENT

The central conflict of Nomad Wars can be summarized as:

> **The world is changing faster than the civilizations can adapt.**

The old rules of migration once allowed different peoples to coexist.

Something has disrupted that balance.

Now the movement of one civilization forces another to move.

Resources become scarce.

Traditional boundaries become unstable.

Migration becomes confrontation.

Confrontation becomes war.

---

# 45. CENTRAL PHILOSOPHY

The ultimate conflict is not simply:

**civilization vs civilization.**

It is:

**civilization vs changing circumstances.**

Civilizations fight one another because they are all attempting to survive within the same changing system.

This creates the fundamental loop of the world:

```text
THE WORLD CHANGES
       ↓
PEOPLE ADAPT
       ↓
ADAPTATION CHANGES THE BALANCE
       ↓
OTHER PEOPLE MUST ADAPT
       ↓
CONFLICT
       ↓
THE WORLD CHANGES AGAIN
```

Therefore:

> **The true antagonist of Nomad Wars is not necessarily a single faction. It is the instability of the world itself.**

---

# 46. DESIGN RULES

The following principles should remain stable unless deliberately revised:

1. The major civilizations once existed within a relatively stable ecological balance.
2. Migration is a survival mechanism, not merely a cultural aesthetic.
3. Environmental change creates resource pressure.
4. Resource pressure creates migration pressure.
5. Migration can create territorial conflict.
6. Conflict can escalate into raids and war.
7. Warfare can directly affect the economy.
8. Horses and livestock are strategic assets.
9. Territory changes in value over time.
10. Mobile settlements make territorial warfare different from conventional RTS warfare.
11. Mobility provides strategic freedom but creates vulnerability.
12. Not every enemy is morally evil.
13. Different civilizations may have legitimate survival motivations.
14. The player cannot completely control the environment.
15. External threats may explain why the old balance collapsed.
16. The exact ultimate cause of the crisis remains open until deliberately established.
17. The main RTS should remain understandable without requiring knowledge of the entire mythology.
18. The world should support future campaigns, factions and games without requiring them for the MVP.
19. Worldbuilding should generate gameplay rather than exist only as background lore.
20. The core philosophy remains:

> **The world changes the players.**

# 49. CANON STATUS

The central conflict structure described here is **world-level canon**.

Specific explanations for the origin of the crisis remain provisional until formally confirmed.

The following principle is currently considered foundational:

> **The civilizations of Nomad Wars are not fighting simply because they are enemies. They are increasingly becoming enemies because the environmental and geopolitical balance that once allowed them to coexist is breaking down.**

The exact force responsible for that breakdown remains one of the major mysteries of the world.
