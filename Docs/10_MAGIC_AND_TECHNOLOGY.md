# 10 — MAGIC AND TECHNOLOGY

**Project:** Nomad Wars  
**Document type:** World Foundation / Worldbuilding  
**Status:** Canon — foundational design  
**Scope:** Magic, technology, their relationship with society, warfare and nomadism

---

## 1. PURPOSE OF THIS DOCUMENT

This document defines the fundamental principles governing:

- magic;
- technology;
- magical and technological development;
- their relationship with the environment;
- their use by different cultures;
- military applications;
- heroes and specialized units;
- the boundary between fantasy and technological explanations.

The purpose is not to create a complete spell list or technology tree.

The purpose is to establish the **rules of the world** from which later gameplay systems, factions, units, buildings, heroes and abilities can be designed.

---

# 2. CORE PRINCIPLE

Magic and technology are not separate game systems that exist only to provide special abilities.

They are parts of civilization.

Different cultures understand and use the forces of the world differently.

One culture may develop:

- metallurgy;
- mechanical devices;
- mobile engineering;
- ranged weapons;
- siege mechanisms.

Another may rely primarily on:

- ritual;
- spirits;
- fire;
- healing;
- environmental knowledge;
- animal relationships.

Another may combine both.

Therefore:

> **The technological and magical identity of a faction should emerge from its culture and way of life.**

The player should be able to understand something about a civilization by looking at its:

- buildings;
- weapons;
- transportation;
- armor;
- rituals;
- magical effects;
- siege equipment;
- heroes.

---

# 3. MAGIC IS REAL

Magic is a real force within the world.

It is not merely superstition or misunderstood technology.

However, magic does not automatically mean that every phenomenon can be explained or controlled.

The existence of magic does not remove the importance of:

- ecology;
- climate;
- geography;
- resources;
- logistics;
- physical limitations;
- human and animal physiology.

Magic exists **inside the world's natural order** rather than replacing it completely.

---

# 4. MAGIC IS NOT UNLIMITED

Magic has limitations.

A civilization cannot simply solve every environmental or military problem with spells.

Powerful effects should require one or more limiting factors, such as:

- trained specialists;
- rare resources;
- time;
- preparation;
- favorable environmental conditions;
- artifacts;
- rituals;
- physical or mental exhaustion;
- specific locations;
- cooperation between several practitioners.

This prevents magic from making the rest of the world irrelevant.

---

# 5. MAGIC AND THE ENVIRONMENT

The world of Nomad Wars is defined by environmental change.

Climate, seasons, resources and migration are fundamental systems.

Magic should therefore interact with the environment rather than exist completely independently from it.

Examples:

- fire magic may be especially effective in dry environments;
- water-related magic may become strategically important during drought;
- cold magic may interact with winter conditions;
- healing or plant-related magic may depend on biological resources;
- spirits may be associated with particular landscapes;
- certain magical resources may appear or disappear as environmental zones move.

These relationships should be designed carefully.

Magic should **enhance the importance of the environment**, not eliminate it.

---

# 6. CULTURAL INTERPRETATION OF MAGIC

Different cultures do not necessarily understand magic in the same way.

One civilization may describe the same phenomenon as:

> spirits.

Another may call it:

> elemental forces.

Another may understand it as:

> divine power.

Another may treat it as:

> a practical technique that can be learned and reproduced.

The underlying phenomenon may be identical while the cultural interpretation differs.

This allows the world to contain different magical traditions without requiring every faction to have completely different laws of magic.

---

# 7. MAGIC AND NOMADISM

Nomadic societies have different requirements from sedentary civilizations.

A magical tradition developed by a mobile civilization should generally favor:

- portability;
- low infrastructure requirements;
- individual practitioners;
- rituals that can be performed during migration;
- animal relationships;
- environmental awareness;
- temporary structures;
- mobile artifacts;
- battlefield flexibility.

Large magical installations may exist, but they should have a reason to remain stationary.

This creates a natural distinction between:

**mobile magic**

and

**territorial magic**.

---

# 8. TECHNOLOGY

Technology develops as a response to environmental and social problems.

Technological development is influenced by:

- available materials;
- geography;
- resources;
- climate;
- warfare;
- animal domestication;
- trade;
- population density;
- cultural traditions;
- magical knowledge.

There is no universal technological progression that every civilization follows.

A faction may be advanced in one field while comparatively primitive in another.

For example:

- exceptional metallurgy but limited architecture;
- sophisticated animal husbandry but simple machines;
- advanced mechanical engineering but weak magical traditions;
- powerful magic but limited industrial production.

---

# 9. TECHNOLOGY AND MOBILE CIVILIZATION

Mobility creates its own technological priorities.

A nomadic civilization values technologies that are:

- lightweight;
- repairable;
- modular;
- transportable;
- adaptable;
- useful during migration;
- compatible with animals and caravans.

Large permanent infrastructure is less attractive unless its strategic value justifies the loss of mobility.

This principle should influence:

- buildings;
- weapons;
- armor;
- transportation;
- storage;
- workshops;
- siege equipment.

---

# 10. ANIMALS AS TECHNOLOGICAL INFRASTRUCTURE

Animals are an important component of civilization.

In Nomad Wars, animals can simultaneously function as:

- food;
- economic resources;
- transportation;
- breeding stock;
- military assets;
- logistical infrastructure.

Horses are particularly important.

A horse is therefore not simply an icon representing a resource.

It can represent:

> mobility + economy + military potential.

Loss of livestock can have consequences beyond food production.

A civilization that loses its horses may lose:

- movement speed;
- cavalry potential;
- logistical capacity;
- ability to migrate efficiently.

---

# 11. HORSES AND MILITARY CAPACITY

At the beginning of the game, horses are intentionally important.

The player may need to:

1. locate wild horses;
2. capture them;
3. accumulate a sufficient herd;
4. establish the necessary infrastructure;
5. use them to support military expansion.

The number and availability of horses can therefore influence the size or composition of the player's military.

This reflects the underlying philosophy:

> **An army is part of a mobile society.**

Military power is not completely independent from the society's economic and ecological condition.

---

# 12. TECHNOLOGY LEVELS

The world should not be divided into simplistic:

> primitive → medieval → advanced

levels.

Technology should instead be **domain-based**.

Possible domains include:

### Metallurgy

- copper/bronze;
- iron;
- advanced alloys;
- magical metallurgy.

### Weapons

- bows;
- spears;
- swords;
- axes;
- specialized ranged weapons;
- mechanical weapons;
- firearms in appropriate cultures.

### Transportation

- horses;
- wagons;
- sledges;
- caravans;
- mobile buildings;
- specialized magical transportation.

### Siege technology

- rams;
- catapults;
- trebuchet-like mechanisms;
- mobile siege structures;
- magical artillery.

### Construction

- wood;
- stone;
- earth;
- bone;
- ivory-like biological materials;
- metal;
- composite structures;
- magical materials.

A faction can therefore be advanced in one domain and conservative in another.

---

# 13. MAGICAL TECHNOLOGY

Magic and technology can interact.

Possible forms include:

- enchanted weapons;
- magical construction;
- magical transportation;
- artifacts;
- elemental weapons;
- enhanced armor;
- magical siege equipment;
- magical communication;
- magical healing.

However:

> Magical technology should require a reason to exist.

It should emerge from the world's cultures, resources and history rather than being added simply because it looks impressive.

---

# 14. FACTIONAL DIFFERENTIATION

Each major faction should have a recognizable relationship between:

**environment → culture → technology → magic → military doctrine.**

For example, a civilization adapted to cold northern environments may develop:

- insulated mobile structures;
- animal-based transportation;
- cold-resistant materials;
- specialized winter equipment;
- magic associated with cold, spirits or endurance.

A civilization adapted to dry southern environments may instead develop:

- water-storage technologies;
- heat-resistant materials;
- desert transportation;
- fire-related magic;
- specialized ranged warfare.

The exact technologies are not fixed by this document.

They should be developed later from the faction documents.

---

# 15. TURAAN / CLASSICAL NOMADIC FACTION

The current MVP faction is the civilization provisionally referred to as:

**Turan**

The name is temporary and may change.

Its technological and magical identity should represent a broader fantasy interpretation of Eurasian nomadic cultures rather than directly reproducing one historical culture.

Potential characteristics include:

- advanced horse culture;
- mobile settlements;
- portable workshops;
- mobile towers;
- mobile command structures;
- strong mounted warfare;
- practical metallurgy;
- mechanical devices;
- bows and ranged weapons;
- potentially early firearms or other specialized ranged technology;
- spiritual or shamanic traditions.

These elements are not all mandatory for the MVP.

They are directions for future development.

---

# 16. MAGIC OF THE TURAAN

The Turan faction should not be defined exclusively by magic.

Magic is one component of its civilization.

Possible magical traditions may include:

- spirits;
- elemental forces;
- fire;
- wind;
- healing;
- protective rituals;
- communication with animals;
- environmental perception.

Heroes may act as particularly powerful representatives of these traditions.

The exact magical system remains to be designed.

---

# 17. HEROES

Heroes are exceptional individuals who combine military ability with specialized powers.

They may differ in:

- combat range;
- mobility;
- durability;
- magical specialization;
- support abilities;
- crowd control;
- reconnaissance;
- leadership.

Heroes are not necessarily the strongest units in every situation.

Their strategic value may come from their ability to influence surrounding units and respond to changing conditions.

---

# 18. HERO AURAS

Heroes may possess **auras**.

An aura affects nearby allied units.

Possible effects include:

- increased attack;
- increased defense;
- movement speed;
- morale;
- resistance;
- regeneration;
- specialized bonuses.

Aura design should reinforce the concept of the hero as a leader rather than simply another powerful soldier.

Positioning therefore becomes important.

A hero accompanying an army is not merely a damage dealer.

The hero can function as a mobile center of influence.

---

# 19. HERO INVENTORY

Heroes may possess an inventory.

The current concept is a limited inventory with approximately:

**9 or more artifact slots.**

Artifacts can modify hero capabilities.

Possible effects include:

- attack;
- defense;
- movement;
- abilities;
- health;
- mana;
- resistance;
- aura effects;
- specialized abilities.

The inventory system should allow different builds for the same hero.

This creates a connection between:

**exploration → resources → artifacts → hero development → strategy.**

---

# 20. HERO ARCHETYPES

Heroes may be designed according to recognizable battlefield roles.

Examples:

### Tank

- high durability;
- close combat;
- protects allied units.

### Fighter

- strong direct damage;
- balanced survivability.

### Assassin

- high mobility;
- high burst damage;
- weak durability.

### Ranged

- attacks from distance;
- vulnerable when surrounded.

### Support

- healing;
- buffs;
- defensive abilities.

### Mage

- powerful area or elemental abilities;
- dependent on mana/resources.

### Scout

- high mobility;
- reconnaissance;
- information about the environment.

These are gameplay archetypes, not necessarily cultural categories.

---

# 21. MILITARY UNIT CLASSIFICATION

Military units may be divided into three broad weight classes.

### LIGHT

Characteristics:

- light armor;
- high movement speed;
- lower individual durability;
- lower or specialized attack power.

Possible roles:

- scouts;
- skirmishers;
- raiders;
- fast infantry;
- light cavalry.

### MEDIUM

Characteristics:

- balanced armor;
- balanced speed;
- balanced attack.

These form the main body of many armies.

### HEAVY

Characteristics:

- heavy armor;
- high durability;
- strong attack;
- low movement speed.

They are valuable in direct combat but less efficient during rapid migration.

This creates a strategic trade-off:

> **Military power versus mobility.**

---

# 22. RANGED UNITS

Ranged units provide damage from distance.

Possible examples:

- archers;
- mounted archers;
- specialized magical ranged units;
- mechanical ranged weapons;
- firearms in appropriate factions.

Ranged units should remain dependent on:

- positioning;
- protection;
- ammunition or resources where appropriate;
- terrain;
- visibility.

---

# 23. SIEGE TECHNOLOGY

Siege units exist to attack structures and fortified positions.

Possible technologies include:

- catapult-like mechanisms;
- mobile siege platforms;
- rams;
- heavy projectile weapons;
- magical siege weapons.

Siege technology should interact strongly with mobile settlements.

A mobile civilization can potentially bring siege equipment to an enemy settlement rather than constructing a permanent siege base.

---

# 24. FLYING UNITS

The fantasy setting allows aerial units.

Flying units may include:

- flying animals;
- magical creatures;
- aerial cavalry;
- specialized flying warriors;
- supernatural creatures.

Flight should not automatically make a unit universally superior.

Flying units can have limitations such as:

- low durability;
- expensive production;
- resource requirements;
- vulnerability to ranged attacks;
- limited carrying capacity;
- faction-specific availability.

Their existence should be justified by the ecology and fantasy rules of the world.

---

# 25. MAGIC DAMAGE

Combat can distinguish between different damage types.

Possible categories include:

- physical;
- magical;
- elemental;
- specialized damage.

Certain units may have resistance or vulnerability to specific damage types.

For example:

- magical units may be more vulnerable to anti-magic attacks;
- heavily armored units may resist physical attacks;
- certain creatures may be vulnerable to fire;
- spirits may interact differently with conventional weapons.

The final damage system belongs to Game Design / Combat documentation.

This document only establishes the world-level principle.

---

# 26. MANA AND MAGICAL CAPACITY

Heroes and magical units may use a limited magical resource such as mana.

Mana should not become simply:

> another gold bar.

Its purpose is to represent limited magical capacity.

Mana can be influenced by:

- hero attributes;
- artifacts;
- abilities;
- environmental conditions;
- magical buildings;
- faction technologies.

Certain anti-magic structures or units may specifically target magical capacity.

---

# 27. MAGICAL BUILDINGS

Magical buildings may function as:

- training facilities;
- research structures;
- ritual centers;
- artifact workshops;
- magical resource processing;
- hero support structures.

Some may be mobile if their cultural and technological logic permits it.

Large permanent magical installations should generally have a strategic reason for remaining stationary.

---

# 28. MOBILE VS STATIONARY TECHNOLOGY

Technology follows the same philosophical distinction as settlements.

### Mobile

Suitable for:

- military camps;
- workshops;
- command structures;
- watchtowers;
- temporary magical structures;
- animal facilities;
- portable siege systems.

### Stationary

Suitable for:

- large mines;
- major temples;
- permanent infrastructure;
- large-scale magical installations;
- specialized industrial facilities;
- structures dependent on local geology.

This distinction reinforces the world's central tension:

> **Mobility provides freedom, but permanence provides efficiency.**

---

# 29. TECHNOLOGICAL TRADE-OFF

A mobile structure should generally sacrifice some efficiency compared with a deployed structure.

For example:

**DEPLOYED**

- stronger defenses;
- higher production;
- higher firing rate;
- better range;
- greater efficiency.

**MOBILE**

- lower efficiency;
- slower movement;
- reduced offensive capability;
- reduced defensive capability.

This is not merely a gameplay balancing rule.

It represents the technological cost of mobility.

---

# 30. TECHNOLOGY AND RESOURCE SCARCITY

Technology requires resources.

Important resources may include:

- wood;
- stone;
- metal;
- gold;
- food;
- animals;
- horses;
- magical resources;
- faction-specific materials.

Rare materials may create strategic specialization.

For example, a faction with access to a rare material may develop technologies unavailable elsewhere.

This creates a relationship between:

**geography → resources → technology → military power.**

---

# 31. ANCIENT TECHNOLOGY AND LOST KNOWLEDGE

The world may contain remnants of older civilizations.

Possible examples:

- ruins;
- abandoned cities;
- ancient artifacts;
- forgotten machines;
- magical structures;
- buried technologies;
- lost weapons.

These can become:

- exploration objectives;
- neutral locations;
- campaign elements;
- sources of artifacts;
- sources of technological upgrades.

The exact history of these technologies belongs to `04_HISTORY_AND_ORIGIN.md`.

---

# 32. EXTERNAL CIVILIZATIONS

The world contains lands beyond the known continent.

The existence of:

- a vast ocean;
- poorly explored territories;
- distant continents;
- island civilizations;

creates opportunities for future civilizations with different technological and magical traditions.

These cultures should not be fully defined until the main world is sufficiently developed.

They can later become:

- new factions;
- campaign antagonists;
- allies;
- DLC civilizations;
- entirely new games set in the same universe.

---

# 33. ANTAGONISTIC FORCES

Future factions may include fundamentally different civilizations or supernatural forces.

Potential directions include:

- undead;
- demons;
- deep-sea civilizations;
- northern civilizations;
- supernatural creatures;
- ancient beings.

These should not be treated simply as reskinned conventional factions.

Their magic, technology, economy and relationship with the environment should emerge from their own world logic.

---

# 34. THE PHILOSOPHICAL PRINCIPLE

The most important rule of magic and technology is:

> **Power should not remove the world's problems. Power should change how civilizations deal with them.**

Magic does not eliminate drought.

Technology does not eliminate migration.

Military power does not eliminate scarcity.

A powerful civilization may simply have more ways to respond.

This preserves the central philosophy of Nomad Wars:

> **The world changes the players.**

The player does not completely control the environment.

The player learns to survive, adapt and exploit changing conditions.

---

# 35. CORE DESIGN EQUATION

The relationship between world systems can be summarized as:

```text
ENVIRONMENT
    ↓
RESOURCES
    ↓
CULTURE
    ↓
TECHNOLOGY + MAGIC
    ↓
SETTLEMENT DESIGN
    ↓
MOBILITY
    ↓
MILITARY DOCTRINE
    ↓
UNITS + HEROES
    ↓
PLAYER STRATEGY
````

The chain works in both directions.

Player actions can temporarily influence:

* resources;
* settlements;
* military power;
* local territory.

But the environment continues to change.

---

# 36. DESIGN RULES

The following rules should remain stable unless deliberately revised:

1. Magic is real but limited.
2. Technology develops from environmental and cultural needs.
3. Different cultures interpret and use magic differently.
4. No civilization must be technologically or magically superior in every field.
5. Mobility is a technological and economic advantage with real costs.
6. Animals are part of the economic and military infrastructure.
7. Horses are strategically important to nomadic societies.
8. Heroes can influence armies through abilities and auras.
9. Artifact inventories allow hero specialization.
10. Light, medium and heavy units create mobility/power trade-offs.
11. Flying units are possible but must have meaningful limitations.
12. Siege technology is an important part of warfare.
13. Magical systems must interact with the world rather than bypass it completely.
14. Permanent infrastructure should generally be more efficient than mobile infrastructure.
15. Magic and technology should reveal the culture that created them.
16. The environment remains a fundamental strategic force.
